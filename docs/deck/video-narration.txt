Muster. An autonomous shift coordinator that knows the law.

First the problem, because it is stranger than it looks. Somewhere right now a manager is writing next week's rota in a spreadsheet. That sounds like nothing. But eleven US jurisdictions regulate how that spreadsheet may be filled in. Fourteen days notice. Premiums when it changes. Extra pay for too little rest. And a worker's right to say no.

And the enforcement is not theoretical.

New York City settled with Starbucks for thirty eight point nine million dollars, after finding more than five hundred thousand violations. Chipotle settled for twenty million. Dunkin, Taco Bell and Walgreens have all settled over the same law.

Those are companies with legal departments. The person I built this for runs three coffee shops and faces the identical rulebook.

Here is what surprised me when I read the ordinances. The rules are not vague. They are arithmetic. Oregon owes one hour at the regular rate to add a shift. Chicago owes a quarter over base for working inside the rest period. New York owes ten, twenty, forty five or seventy five dollars depending on the notice given.

Every one computable. Almost nobody computes them.

Because the moment it matters is six in the morning. A hole in the rota, four legal ways to fill it, and two of them need someone's permission. Nobody has time to work that out at six.

So Muster does the deciding.

It drafts next week under the law, then holds that week together. A shift loses its worker, and Muster finds every lawful cover, prices each against that city's ordinance, and takes the cheapest. Every change writes a receipt naming the clause, the money, and who decided.

The same sick call, same hour, same wage, in three cities. Eighteen dollars in Portland. Nothing in Chicago. Seventy five in New York.

The rulebook is data, not code. Every figure carries the government source it was read from.

You decide how much rope it gets. But two rules ignore that dial completely.

Muster will never waive a worker's rest period on their behalf, and never asks a worker who has already said no. At every level, including the most permissive.

That is not my opinion about agents. Oregon requires the worker's own consent, New York requires it in writing, and Chicago's guidance says the Right to Rest has no exceptions. An agent that could dial past those would not be more capable. It would be unlawful.

Strands Agents runs two loops on Bedrock. Claude reasons and never does the arithmetic: the premiums, the rest remedies and the totals are pure functions, which is why they are testable. It also ships an MCP server over the compliance engine, read only, so another agent can ask what a change costs but cannot change anyone's week.

Two hundred and twenty six tests run offline, and it is live on AWS right now.

This is the console. Three coffee shops, in Oregon, Chicago and New York.

Schedule is the week. The panel at the top is cover against demand for the day I pick: the filled area is what the day needs, the line is who is scheduled, and where the line drops below it we are short. Underneath is the rota, coloured by role.

Decisions is the only place Muster interrupts me. Everything else it handled on its own.

Consent is the asks out with workers, and what they answered. A decline is final.

People is the roster: each person's rate, the hours they were promised, their certifications, and who is on the standby list. That list exists only in Oregon, because Oregon is the only one of these cities that offers the carve-out.

Autonomy is the dial, and every rule that can stop the agent.

Timeline is every model call and tool call, written by hooks, so the agent never reports on itself.

And Receipts is what actually happened, exportable as a CSV, because that is the file an investigator asks for.
